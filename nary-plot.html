    How points with more than 4 dimensions are placed

    A triangle or tetrahedron can hold 3 or 4 dimensions exactly, with no ambiguity: every point inside corresponds to exactly one composition, and vice versa. Past 4 dimensions there's no way to keep that exactness in 3D space — 3 coordinates just can't losslessly encode 4+ numbers that sum to 1. So for 5+ dimensions, this plot switches to a different, well-known technique called RadViz (radial visualization, also called a "spring" or "star" layout): each dimension gets its own anchor point spread around a sphere, and a composition's position is the weighted average of those anchors — as if each anchor pulled the point toward it with a spring whose strength is that dimension's value. RadViz was originally described for 2D (anchors on a circle); what's shown here for n≥5 is the natural 3D extension (anchors on a sphere instead of a circle).

    The tradeoff is real: RadViz is a many-to-one projection, so distinct compositions can end up looking close together or even identical in position — it's good for an at-a-glance feel for where a composition sits, not for reading exact values off the picture (that's what the calculation panel above is for; it's always exact, regardless of N).
